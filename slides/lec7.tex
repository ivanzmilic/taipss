\section{Intro}

\frame{
	\frametitle{Recap}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Now, we understand the main processes that govern stellar structure \emph{(not yet evolution!)}.
		\item To recap our main assumptions: 
		\item Stars are in hydrostatic equilibrium. 
		\item Energy is transported either by radiation or convection
		\item Hopefully we understand equation of state (connection between $T, \rho, P$)
		\item Hopefully we can calculate the opacity from there
		\item And hopefully we can calculate the rate of energy production.
	\end{itemize}
}

\frame{
\frametitle{Equations of stellar structure}
Here they are,  written in terms of $r$ as independent variable:
\begin{align} 
	\frac{dP}{dr} &= -\rho \frac{Gm}{r^2} \\
	\frac{dm}{dr} &= 4\pi r^2 \rho \\
	\frac{dT}{dr} &= -\frac{3}{4ac}\frac{\kappa \rho}{T^3} \frac{F}{4\pi r^2} \\
	\frac{dF}{dr} &= 4\pi r^2 \rho q \\
	P &= \frac{{\mathcal R}}{\mu_I} \rho T + P_e + \frac{1}{3}a T^4 \\
	\kappa &= \kappa_0 \rho^a T^b \\
	q &= q_0 \rho^m T^n 
\end{align}
}

\frame{
\frametitle{Equations of stellar structure}
Four of these equations are differential equations. Three are algebraic. Differential equations are of the first order, so each one needs one boundary condition. 
These are, generally speaking. 
\begin{itemize} 
	\item At $r=0$ (center), $m=0$, $F=0$. 
	\item At $m=M$, $P=0$ and $F=L$.
\end{itemize}
It turns out that two physical parameters that govern the structure a so-called ZAMS (zero age main sequence star) star are the \textbf{total mass} and \textbf{chemical composition}. More about that in the next class.

\q{Can someone tell me why we could not say at $r=R$, $m=M$ and $P=0$ and be done with it?}

Because we do not know what $R$ is. When solving the equations of stellar structure, we don't know in advance how big the star will be!
}
%
\frame{
\frametitle{General assumptions}
To ease our job, some simple things can, generally be assumed
\begin{itemize} 
	\item Constant $\mu$, either for ZAMS or completely evolved stars. 
	\item When $q=$, $F$ is constant. 
	\item $P$ and $\rho$ and $T$ can all be safely assumed to monotoneously decrease with $r$ ($m$).
	\item It is super helpful if we can find combinations of quantities that stay approximately constant over the stellar radius. 
\end{itemize}
Now - be mindful that the situation can be even more dire - as the opacity and nuclear rate production can be very complicated and no analytical solution is possible.

\textbf{In modeling, in general, we solve everything by numerics.}
}

\frame{
\frametitle{Further simplifications}
\q{What would I need to close the system?} 

Equation of state! But such an equation of state, where $T$ does not appear, that is where $P$ depends only on $\rho$ ($\mu$ can also appear as it is a given constant, but discuss this for a sec).
\begin{align} 
	\frac{dP}{dr} &= -\rho \frac{Gm}{r^2} \nonumber\\
	\frac{dm}{dr} &= 4\pi r^2 \rho \nonumber \\
	%\frac{dT}{dr} &= -\frac{3}{4ac}\frac{\kappa \rho}{T^3} \frac{F}{4\pi r^2} \nonumber \\
	%\frac{dF}{dr} &= 4\pi r^2 \rho q \nonumber \\
	%P &= \frac{{\mathcal R}}{\mu_I} \rho T + P_e + \frac{1}{3}a T^4 \nonumber \\
	%\kappa &= \kappa_0 \rho^a T^b \nonumber \\
	%q &= q_0 \rho^m T^n \nonumber
	P &= K \rho^\gamma
\end{align}
Where we typically define $n$ as $\gamma = 1+1/n$.

So, now we have closed the system of equations, and we can go to town! :-)

The ensuing models are called \textbf{polytropes}, and can be solved analytically only in a handful of cases (for specific $n$'s) - as you have seen in your homework.
}

Multiply first equation by $\frac{r^2}{\rho}$, differentiate with respect to $dr$ and substitute into the equation two, you will get:

\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{r^2} \frac{d}{dr} (\frac{r^2}{\rho} \frac{dP}{dr}) = - 4 \pi G \rho
\end{equation}
And good old: $P = K \rho^\gamma$
}
