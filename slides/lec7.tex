\section{Intro}

\frame{
	\frametitle{Recap}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Now, we understand the main processes that govern stellar structure \emph{(not yet evolution!)}.
		\item To recap our main assumptions: 
		\item Stars are in hydrostatic equilibrium. 
		\item Energy is transported either by radiation or convection
		\item Hopefully we understand equation of state (connection between $T, \rho, P$)
		\item Hopefully we can calculate the opacity from there
		\item And hopefully we can calculate the rate of energy production.
	\end{itemize}
}

\frametitle{Equations of stellar structure}
Here they are,  written in terms of $r$ as independent variable:
\begin{align} 
	\frac{dP}{dr} &= -\rho \frac{Gm}{r^2} \\
	\frac{dm}{dr} &= 4\pi r^2 \rho \\
	\frac{dT}{dr} &= -\frac{3}{4ac}\frac{\kappa \rho}{T^3} \frac{F}{4\pi r^2} \\
	\frac{dF}{dr} &= 4\pi r^2 \rho q \\
	P &= \frac{{\mathcal R}}{\mu_I} \rho T + P_e + \frac{1}{3}a T^4 \\
	\kappa &= \kappa_0 \rho^a T^b \\
	q &= q_0 \rho^m T^n 
\end{align}

\frame{
\frametitle{General assumptions}
To ease our job, some simple things can, generally be assumed
\begin{itemize} 
	\item Constant $\mu$, either for ZAMS or completely evolved stars. 
	\item When $q=$, $F$ is constant. 
	\item $P$ and $\rho$ and $T$ can all be safely assumed to monotoneously decrease with $r$ ($m$).
	\item It is super helpful if we can find combinations of quantities that stay approximately constant over the stellar radius. 
\end{itemize}
Now - be mindful that the situation can be even more dire - as the opacity and nuclear rate production can be very complicated and no analytical solution is possible.

\textbf{In modeling, in general, we solve everything by numerics.}
}

\begin{align} 
	\frac{(n+1)K}{4\pi G n} \frac{1}{r^2} \frac{d}{dr} (\frac{r^2}{\rho^{(n-1)/n}}\frac{d\rho}{dr}) = -\rho \nonumber
\end{align}

We have a second order differential equation, with only one unknown (need two boundary conditions)!

This is a two-point boundary problem, these often arise in stellar physics. Namely, we have two different boundary conditions at two different points. 

For example: $(\frac{d\rho}{dr})_{r=0} = 0$ and $(\rho)_{r=R} = 0$.

This gives rise to additional complications. But today we will not actually work on the solution.
}

\frame{
\frametitle{Sketching the solution}

\begin{align} 
	\frac{(n+1)K}{4\pi G n} \frac{1}{r^2} \frac{d}{dr} (\frac{r^2}{\rho^{(n-1)/n}}\frac{d\rho}{dr}) = -\rho \nonumber
\end{align}

We have a second order differential equation, with only one unknown (need two boundary conditions)!

The complete solution is determined by $K,n, R$ ($R$ is not ideal, but we can ``tune'' it so that $R$ reproduces desired mass). Once we solve the problem we can calculate everything from $\rho$. Even the equations that we did not start from (e.g. Flux).

}
%
\frame{
\frametitle{Sketching the solution}

Additional steps in the solution are, we define $\theta$, which serves as a scaling factor:
\begin{equation}
\rho = \rho_c \theta^n
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
\left [ \frac{(n+1)K}{4\pi G \rho_c^{(n-1)/n}} \right ] = \alpha ^2
\end{equation}
which defines: 
\begin{equation}
r = \alpha \xi
\end{equation}
And leads to the \textbf{Lane - Emden equation of index $n$}:
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{\xi^2} \frac{d}{d\xi} (\xi^2 \frac{d\theta}{d \xi}) = -\theta ^ n
\end{equation}
With boundaries: $\theta =1$, $\frac{d\theta}{d\xi} =0$ at $\xi=0$. 


}
%
\frame{
\frametitle{Lane - Emden equation}

\begin{minipage}{0.48\linewidth}

\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{\xi^2} \frac{d}{d\xi} (\xi^2 \frac{d\theta}{d \xi}) = -\theta ^ n
\end{equation}
With boundaries: $\theta =1$, $\frac{d\theta}{d\xi} =0$ at $\xi=0$. 

Now, we can integrate numerically from the center outwards. Solutions with $n<5$ decrease monotonically and have zero at a finite $\xi$ (not guaranteed), which then defines the stellar radius.

\frame{
\frametitle{Mass-Radius relationship}

After solving for given $n$, we can integrate what we need to find the total mass radius, etc. 

Important ensuing relationship is:
\begin{equation}
\frac{GM}{M_n}^{n-1} \frac{R}{R_n}^{3-n} = \frac{[(n+1) K]^n}{4\pi G}
\end{equation}
Where $R_n$ and $M_n$ are constants for given $n$ (see table 5.1 in the book).

Two special cases $n=3$ and $n=1$. 

\q{Why? Discuss!}
}
%
\frame{
\frametitle{Mass-Radius relationship}

\begin{equation}
\frac{GM}{M_n}^{n-1} \frac{R}{R_n}^{3-n} = \frac{[(n+1) K]^n}{4\pi G}
\end{equation}

Two special cases $n=3$ and $n=1$. 

For $n=3$ mass is independent of radius of the star and only depends on K. 

For $n=1$ radius is independent of mass of the star and only depends on K. 

But in between, we have the following:
\begin{equation}
R^{3-n} \propto \frac{1}{M^{n-1}}
\end{equation}

Both exponents are positive, so more massive stars are smaller!

They also then have higher central pressures, burn faster, and eventually live shorter (need some imagination, but it's true).
}
%
\frame{
\frametitle{Pressure - density relationship in the center}

For completeness (look at the book for details).

If we now express $K$ through $M$ and $R$, and ``compress' some constants, we can get:
\begin{equation}
P_c = {\rm const} B_n M^{2/3} \rho_c^{4/3}
\end{equation}
$B_n$ itself turns out to vary slowly with $n$, so the relationship is almost universal. We will use it soon to discuss the stellar evolution. 
}

\section{Chandrasekhar limit}
\frame{
\frametitle{Degenerate gas}

Polytropes with $n=1.5$ do exist. These are the stars supported by degenerate pressure of electrons. So called white dwarves. They are extremely dense: $10^5 \times$ solar density.

For $n=1.5$, we get:
\begin{equation}
R \propto M^{-1/3}
\end{equation}
implying:
\begin{equation}
\overline{\rho} \propto M^2
\end{equation}
Meaning that some average density of the star increases quadratically with mass. 

This compression eventually causes the gas to become \emph{relativistic}, and the polytrope to shift to $n=3$.
}
\frame{
\frametitle{Relativistic degenerate gas}

This compression eventually causes the gas to become \emph{relativistic}, and the polytrope to shift to $n=3$.

But for $n=3$ we have had, according to:

\begin{equation}
\frac{GM}{M_n}^{n-1} \frac{R}{R_n}^{3-n} = \frac{[(n+1) K]^n}{4\pi G}
\end{equation}

$M$ has only one value, independent of $R$. 

The interpretation for this is tricky.

We can say that there is an upper limit for the mass at which the gas can reach $n=3$. Smaller masses do not reach exactly $n=3$, but higher masses are \emph{impossible}.

\q{Are they really impossible?}
}

\textbf{Not quite - they are impossible for the assumed equation of state. More dense structures exist, these are named neutron stars.}
}

\frame{
\frametitle{Chandraksehar}

\begin{minipage}{0.48\linewidth}
Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar (1910 - 1985), was Indian-American astrophysicist, one of the fathers of this theory and a nobel laureate (shared with Fowler).

He did the derivation above (more or less), and finally concluded that the limiting mass is, in physical units:
\begin{equation}
M_{\rm Ch} = 5.83 \mu_e^{-2} M_{\odot}
\end{equation}
Now for a star consisting of He, C, O, purely ionized $\mu_e = 2$, and limiting mass is 1.46 solar masses. 

For iron, $\mu_e = 2.15$, and the mass is 1.26 solar masses.

\frametitle{Including radiative energy transport}

We will now include radiative transport in our modeling:
\begin{align} 
	\frac{dP}{dr} &= -\rho \frac{Gm}{r^2} \nonumber\\
	%\frac{dm}{dr} &= 4\pi r^2 \rho \nonumber \\
	\frac{dT}{dr} &= -\frac{3}{4ac}\frac{\kappa \rho}{T^3} \frac{F}{4\pi r^2} \nonumber \\
	%\frac{dF}{dr} &= 4\pi r^2 \rho q \nonumber \\
	%P &= \frac{{\mathcal R}}{\mu_I} \rho T + P_e + \frac{1}{3}a T^4 \nonumber \\
	%\kappa &= \kappa_0 \rho^a T^b \nonumber \\
	%q &= q_0 \rho^m T^n \nonumber
	P_{\rm rad} &= \frac{1}{3} a T^4
\end{align}
leads to: 
\begin{equation}
\frac{dP_{\rm rad}}{dP} = \frac{\kappa F}{4 \pi c G m}
\end{equation}
}
As radiation pressure cannot be larger than the total pressure - it means there is a limit to the radiative flux of the star!
%
\frame{
\frametitle{Eddington Luminosity}

 \begin{equation}
\frac{dP_{\rm rad}}{dP} = \frac{\kappa F}{4 \pi c G m}
\end{equation}
}
As radiation pressure cannot be larger than the total pressure - it means there is a limit to the radiative flux of the star, that is:
\begin{equation}
k F < 4 \pi c G m
\end{equation}
Or at the surface: 
\begin{equation}
L < \frac{4 \pi c G M}{\kappa}
\end{equation}
which leads to definition of so called Eddington luminosity:
\begin{equation}
L_{\rm Edd} = 3.2 \times 10^4 \frac{M}{M_{\odot}} (\frac{\kappa_{es}}{\kappa}) L_{\odot}
\end{equation}
\textbf{Important:} As there is another connection between $L$ and $M$, this puts a limit on the maximum ass of the star. It is indeed the case. Very massive stars can exist, but live shortly and lose \textbf{a lot} of mass through stellar wind.
